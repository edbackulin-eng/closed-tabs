TITLE: The 360,000 Dollar Ghost Console
THUMBNAIL: SFX-100
===SCRIPTS===
> Two hundred prototypes were built.
[pause 0.7]
< Then every single one of them was supposed to have been destroyed.

[pause 0.4]
> Except one.
< Sitting in a dusty box.
< Inside an attic.

[pause 0.7]
How did a machine that should not exist end up changing the entire history of video games?
[pause 0.7]
The answer is stranger than you think.
[pause 0.4]
And it starts in the 1990s.

> The Super Nintendo Entertainment System was a massive hit.
Outselling every rival in the 16-bit generation.
< But the ground was shifting.

[pause 0.4]
Compact discs were arriving.
Offering massive storage, full-motion video, and crystal-clear audio.
Competitors like NEC and Sega were already releasing CD add-ons.
[pause 0.7]
Nintendo needed a counter-punch.
So they turned to Sony.

> A Sony engineer named Ken Kutaragi had secretly designed the sound chip for the SNES.
Working without full corporate approval.
Nearly getting fired for his trouble.
< But he had the backing of a top executive.
And he was convinced that CD-ROMs were the future.

Kutaragi pitched a CD-ROM drive for the Super Nintendo.
Nintendo was skeptical at first, worried about slow load times.
< But they agreed to let development begin in late 1988.
With one condition: Kutaragi claimed the drive was for multimedia, not games.

> The result was a Sony-branded hybrid console called the PlayStation.
It had a slot for Super Nintendo cartridges.
And a tray-loading CD drive using a proprietary format called the Super Disc.
< Early proposals even discussed adding the Super FX coprocessor.
Allowing for rudimentary 3D graphics right out of the box.

[pause 0.7]
But behind closed doors, a corporate war was brewing.

> Sony wanted total control.
They demanded the rights to the Super Disc format, software licensing, and all music and movie content.
< Nintendo president Hiroshi Yamauchi was furious.

Yamauchi suspected Nintendo was being used to launch Sony's own gaming empire.
He found the terms completely unacceptable.
[pause 0.7]
So he hatched a secret plan.

> Behind Sony's back, Yamauchi sent his executives to the Netherlands.
To negotiate a deal with Sony's arch-rival: Philips.
< The goal was simple.
Give Nintendo its software control back.
< And gracefully destroy Sony's ambitions.

[pause 0.7]
The betrayal was executed with surgical precision.

> Just two days before the Consumer Electronics Show in May 1991, a newspaper report broke the news.
Nintendo was partnering with Philips.
< Then, at CES in June 1991, Sony proudly unveiled the PlayStation to the public.
The very next day, Nintendo announced Philips.

[pause 0.4]
Journalists would later call it the greatest betrayal in video game history.

> But that was not the strangest part.
Even after the public knife-in-the-back, the partnership didn't die instantly.
< Two to three hundred prototypes of the Sony PlayStation were actually produced.
Carrying the model number SFX-100.

For a brief window, the two corporate giants kept talking.
In early 1992, they even reached a deal.
Sony could make the hardware, while Nintendo kept the game profits.
[pause 0.7]
< But the blood had already been poisoned.

Kutaragi persuaded his company to walk away from Nintendo entirely.
To drop SNES compatibility.
And to build a standalone, next-generation machine.
< In May 1992, Sony cut all ties.
Scrapping the prototypes and leaving Nintendo in the dust.

[pause 0.7]
What happened to all those machines?
[pause 0.4]
They were supposed to have been tossed into the scrap heap.
Erased from timelines.

[pause 0.7]
Instead, one of them vanished into the corporate ether.

> Fast forward to a bankruptcy auction in 2009.
A former worker at a company called Advanta acquired a lot of items.
Among them: an abandoned console left behind by a former chief executive.
< The box sat in an attic for years.
Unopened.
Unnoticed.
< Until it was pulled out into the light.

[pause 0.7]
What did the find reveal?

> A machine bearing two Super NES controller ports.
A cartridge slot.
A tray-loading dual-speed CD-ROM drive.
RCA composite jacks, S-Video, and a headphone jack on the front.
< A serial port labelled "NEXT" for debugging.
And a mysterious expansion port underneath.

It played Super Famicom cartridges and an included test cartridge.
< But there was a catch.
It was missing its original power supply.
And the audio output and CD drive were completely dead.

[pause 0.7]
Could it be saved?

> In 2016, the owner handed the ghost console to a hardware hacker.
Who posted a complete tear-down video for the world to see.
< The hacker discovered internal faults in several components.
Replaced them.
And built a custom power supply using a modified Sony Walkman connector.

[pause 0.7]
The impossible happened.
< The machine came back to life.
Playing game cartridges and audio CDs on hardware that officially never existed.

[pause 0.7]
But programmers wanted more.
Since no original game CDs for the prototype had ever been found, a German duo decided to create one.
< They built a homebrew game titled *Super Boss Gaiden*.
A 16-bit platformer where a fictional corporate boss goes on a rampage through Sony's headquarters.
Because his executives let the last SNES PlayStation prototype fall into the hands of the public.

[pause 0.7]
A game about the machine.
Running on the machine.

[pause 0.4]
In February 2020, the prototype went to auction.
Starting at fifteen thousand dollars.
Within two days, bidding skyrocketed.
< It finally sold for three hundred and sixty thousand dollars.
Winning a Guinness World Record for the most expensive videogame console sold online.

[pause 0.7]
Today, the buyer plans to put it in a permanent museum.
While Ken Kutaragi himself revealed in 2025 that he had quietly kept another pristine unit hidden in his closet all along.

[pause 0.7]
Two machines out of three hundred.
Surviving from a timeline that was cancelled before it began.

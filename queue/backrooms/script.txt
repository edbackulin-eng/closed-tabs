> A room that does not exist anymore started one of the biggest horror legends on the internet.
< It was a real room. In a real building. And for five years, nobody knew where.
> The photo that began it all was taken in 2002.
> Years before the internet had a name for it. Years before anyone called it the Backrooms.
< So where was it taken? Who found it? And what happened to the room?
> That answer is coming. But first, you need to see what it grew into.

> May twelfth, 2019.
> An anonymous user opens a thread on 4chan.
> The board is /x/. The paranormal board.
The request is simple. Post disquieting images. Pictures that just feel "off."
> And attached to the thread is a photograph.
Picture it.
A large, empty room.
Carpet. Yellow wallpaper. Fluorescent lights overhead.
The camera is tilted, just slightly. A Dutch angle.
< Nothing is wrong with it. And that is exactly what is wrong with it.

> Then another user replies.
They give the place a name. And they write a description.

< "If you're not careful and you noclip out of reality in the wrong areas, you'll end up in the Backrooms."
< "Where it's nothing but the stink of old moist carpet, the madness of mono-yellow, the endless background noise of fluorescent lights at maximum hum-buzz."
< "And approximately six hundred million square miles of randomly segmented empty rooms to be trapped in."

< "God save you if you hear something wandering around nearby, because it sure as hell has heard you."

> Now, what happens next is the strange part.
Not a single company made this. No author. No studio.
< Within days, people were writing their own Backrooms stories.
> They posted them on Reddit. On subreddits for creepypasta. Then on a subreddit just for the Backrooms.
And they kept building.
> Somebody imagined a second floor. Then a third.
Writers started calling them "levels."
Each level had its own rules, its own look, its own feel.
> And then came the entities.
Hostile things that roam the rooms.
Nobody was in charge. Anyone could add something.
[pause 0.6]
> One magazine account of the early lore describes it like this.
Level zero is the one from the photo.
Yellow walls. Damp carpet. Humming lights.
Level one swaps the carpet and walls for concrete. The lights flicker. Machinery echoes somewhere.
Level two? The halls get narrow. The heat climbs.
That same account says few ever reach it.
> Remember, none of this is canon.
It is a pile of stories. Written by strangers. Stacked on top of each other.
And that created a problem.
[pause 0.5]
> Because when everyone can add to a story, who decides what counts?
< The fandom actually split over it.
> As new levels kept piling up, one group of fans said: enough.
They wanted the original. Just the original room.
So they started their own subreddit, called r/TrueBackrooms, for the version that came first.
> One news outlet pointed out the core difficulty.
Unlike fandoms built around a movie or a book, there was no official version.
That made it hard to draw a line between real storytelling and jokes.
> Even so, the main subreddit kept growing.
< By March 2022, it had over 157,000 members.
> And it spread everywhere.
YouTube. Twitter. TikTok.
Discord servers.
Wikis, too, on Fandom and Wikidot, where editors write the lore together.
Those wikis work something like the SCP Foundation wiki.
> The creator of the TV series Severance later listed the Backrooms among his many influences.
A photo on a paranormal board, reaching a television writers' room.

> But that was not the strangest part.

< Where was the photo from?

Nothing but the picture.

> They traced the image to an archived webpage.
Not a new one. An old one.
< A page from March 2003, preserved on the Wayback Machine.
[pause 0.5]
> It was a renovation blog.
Run by a branch of a hobby shop chain, HobbyTown, in Oshkosh, Wisconsin.

A former furniture store.
For much of the twentieth century, a business called Rohner's Home Furnishings had filled that building.
Then, in 2003, a new tenant took over part of it.
That was the hobby shop.
> The upper floor had been renovated sometime in 2002.

On March second, 2003, those photos went up on the blog.
> One of them had the file name "Dsc00161.jpg."
< That was the Backrooms photo.

< A room that never had a view of the outside.

< The description did not come from the photo. A commenter imagined the smell.

> So what became of the real room?
< It no longer exists.
> The interior as shown was converted into a radio-controlled car racing track.
Think about that for a second.
The most famous empty room on the internet was turned into a place for toy cars.
Small, fast, noisy.
[pause 0.6]
> But the story of the building was not finished.
By 2024, the building needed work.
The roof had fallen into disrepair.
> And when the connection to the Backrooms came out, the owner had an idea.
Start a crowdfunding campaign. Save the building.
They also considered selling Backrooms memorabilia.
Including samples of the original wallpaper and carpet.
> The renovation was estimated at about seventy-eight thousand dollars.
< The campaign raised more than twenty-four thousand.
That is roughly a third of the goal.
Not enough to cover it. But a lot of money for a room people only knew as a joke.
> In 2025, the building and the store were sold.
The shop still operates today, under a new name.

> So the mystery of the photo is solved.
But a bigger question remains.
< Why did this picture work?
[pause 0.5]
> There is no single answer. But critics have offered some.
One technology outlet said the Backrooms stood out from other creepypastas for two reasons.
It was collaborative.
And it had no overt horror, no obvious threat.
> A professor of internet studies agreed with another outlet on something.
The Backrooms is scary because it invites you to interpret what is not shown.
< You fill in the gaps. And you fill them with your own fears.
> One of them said the horror comes partly from a subtle wrongness.
A place that feels oddly familiar.
Offices. Waiting rooms. Hallways.
With the people and the purpose removed.
> That idea has a name now. Liminal spaces.
Some sources believe the Backrooms was where the aesthetic began.
Others would argue it is just the best known example.
< The hashtag #liminalspaces has nearly one hundred million views on TikTok.
[pause 0.6]
> And what about that word, "noclip"?
One magazine traces it to the video game Doom II.
A player could type a cheat code and walk through walls, outside the intended map.
> The magazine explains that, today, the word has a wider meaning.
Real or digital glitches where the walls of reality fall away, even for a split second.
So in the Backrooms, you don't walk in through a door.
< You fall through a gap in the world.
[pause 0.6]
> Some people took that literally.

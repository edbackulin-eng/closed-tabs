> There was a children's show on a small local channel in the early 1970s.
> Marionette pirates. A little girl named Janice. Creepy calliope music.
< And it never existed.
[pause 0.7]
> Not a lost tape. Not a missing broadcast. It was never made.
> But thousands of strangers have discussed it online as if it were a shared memory.
< So how does a show nobody watched become a show everybody remembers?
> The answer is stranger than a ghost story. And it starts with a forum thread.

Let's go back to 2009.
A horror writer posts a short story on his own website.
The story is not told as a story. It is told as a conversation.

> The setting is a message board called NetNostalgia Forum.
> The kind of place where adults go to ask, "Does anyone remember...?"
A user asks about an old children's show.
It was called Candle Cove.
It aired on Channel 58, in the early 1970s.
The users say they come from the Huntington–Ashland area.
One version of the story's retelling puts the show on a local channel in Ironton, Ohio.
Even the retellings can't agree on the details. Hold on to that.

> Here's how the thread begins.
< Someone remembers the main character. A young girl named Janice.
She has imaginary adventures. With marionette pirates.
> Then the others start to chip in.
There's Pirate Percy. A cowardly buccaneer.
He captains a ship called the Laughingstock.
< And the ship is alive. It has a wide mouth across its bow.
[pause 0.5]
Someone remembers what the ship says to the frightened Percy.
Over and over.
< "You have... to go... inside."
> Doesn't that sound like a children's show?
> A little odd, maybe. A little cheap. But harmless.
Keep reading.

> Another user remembers a villain.
A one-eyed pirate named Horace Horrible.
A handlebar mustache. And a set of teeth that are too tall.
And then someone remembers the worst of them.
A skeleton in a tattered cape and top hat.
< He calls himself the Skin-Taker.
In the thread, a user says the skeleton wore clothing made from the skin of children.
[pause 0.7]
> Wait. Was that really on a children's show?
> Nobody in the thread seems sure.
That's the trick of it.
Every memory sounds slightly wrong. Slightly too much.
And each new commenter agrees with it anyway.

> There's more.
Someone recalls an episode where nothing happens.
No story. No adventure.
< The characters just scream. The whole episode.
Flailing, and screaming.
And the people in the thread are not sure whether they saw that.
Or whether they only feel as though they did.
Does that ever happen to you?
You remember a scene from childhood, and it's wrong. Too dark. Too strange.
And you can't tell where it came from.

> But that is not the part that made this story famous.
> The last post is.
< A user writes that he recently talked with his mother about the show.
He told her he used to watch Candle Cove all the time.
She didn't remember the show.
< But she remembered something else.
Whenever he told her he was watching Candle Cove,
the television was showing nothing.
< Nothing but static.
[pause 1.0]
That's it. That is the ending.
No monster arrives. No one explains.
A pirate ship with a mouth. A skeleton in a top hat. A screaming episode.
And a child, sitting in front of a screen full of static, seeing all of it.

> So what was he watching?
> Or what was he making up?
We'll come back to that.
But first, I want to tell you where the story came from.
Because the origin is almost funny.

> The author is a web cartoonist named Kris Straub.
> He wrote it for his website, Ichor Falls.
A site that collected his horror stories.
All set in a fictional town with that name.
The story was published in 2009, under a Creative Commons license.
That matters. Remember it.

> And the inspiration?
< A joke.
Straub said in a 2011 interview that the idea came from a satirical article in The Onion.
The headline: "Area 36-Year-Old Still Has Occasional Lidsville Nightmare."
Lidsville was a children's television series from the 1970s.
One account says the article ran in 2000.
It was meant as a gag. A grown man, still shaken by a kids' show.
> But Straub read it and thought something else.
He said it was funny.
But that the basic concept was frighteningly true.

Here is how he put it.
"So many things that scare us as kids start from this innocuous desire to entertain children."
"But it's produced carelessly, or some special effect comes out way more ponderous or ugly than the creators intended."
"And it lingers as we, as children, try to make it fit with our limited understanding of the world."
[pause 0.7]
< Think about that.
A show doesn't need to be a horror to terrify a child.
It only needs to be strange. And a child's mind does the rest.
Straub said he wrote the story just to get the idea out of his head.

> He expected a short story.
> What he got was a legend.

> Because then, the story escaped.
Fans began copying and pasting it.
Without his approval, they carried it to bigger horror fiction sites.
It landed on creepypasta.com and the Creepypasta Wiki, the two largest repositories.
It showed up on 4chan. On Reddit. On YouTube. On IGN. On horror.com.
> That's the word, by the way. Creepypasta.
A neologism for horror stories that circulate around the internet.
Cut and pasted, again and again,
until they take on a life of their own.

< And then came the strangest step.
On some forums, fans recreated the story's exchange.
They didn't just share the thread.
They posted it as though it were actually happening.
[pause 0.7]
> Think what that means.
A person logs on and finds a stranger asking, "Does anyone remember Candle Cove?"
Another replies, "Yes. The skeleton. The ship."
A third remembers the screaming episode.
It is a story, performed live, with real people as the cast.
And it was often shared as some kind of urban legend.
Detached from Straub. Detached from its source.

> Here's what I find remarkable.
The story was about a false memory shared by a group.
And then it became one.
Not a true one. Nobody was claiming to have seen the show in 1972.
But a memory in the ordinary sense of the word.
Something a lot of people carry, and can describe.

> And the details kept growing.
A fan wiki now holds the show's characters and mythology.
All of it fleshed out and expanded by fans.
Detailed episode descriptions.
For two seasons of the show.
< Two seasons. Of a show that doesn't exist.
> And there's more.
The wiki explores a book called The Nickerbocker's Tale, dated 1767.
Supposedly, it inspired the made-up show.
< That book is also a fan invention.
A fiction inside a fiction, explaining a fiction.
[pause 0.7]
There's fan art, all over the internet.
There are videos and music about the show.
And some of those videos claim to be footage of the show itself.
Screaming included. One account warns you to keep the volume low.
There are videos of people reading original stories.
Their own imagined experiences of watching Candle Cove as children.
And, yes, there's even a mashup with the web series Kids React.
> Peak internet.

> So, what did Straub think?
You might expect anger.
His story was copied everywhere, without asking.
But in the 2011 interview he said he saw it as a flattering evolution.
He said the natural thing for such a story to do is be taken up by the shadowy corners of the internet.
And he said that serves the legend far more than being strict about it would.
Though he also made clear he owns the copyright.
< And he doesn't like the idea of people making money from his work without permission.
That's a fair line. Share the story. Don't sell it.

> There was one more difference, and critics noticed it.
Most creepypasta, said one writer at The Verge, has an "anonymous folkloric quality."
Nobody knows who wrote them. They seem to come from nowhere.
< Candle Cove did not.
It came from a known author, and a known website.
You can find the original, and the date.
And still, it behaves like folklore.

> Which brings us to the question.
> Why did this one stick?

Let's take the best explanations we have.
Not one answer. A few.

> Theory one. The format.
A writer at Aeon called it among the best creepypastas out there.
And a good example of using the internet forum as a way to tell a story.
Think about what a forum is.
Many voices. No narrator. Nobody in charge.
Each commenter adds a detail, and the next one agrees.
That is exactly how real memories get built in a group.
There is no author in the story, so you never feel the author's hand.

> Theory two. The nostalgia.
The same Verge writer called the story a perfectly dark spin on our nostalgia for the half-remembered stories of our childhood.
The realization that the things we liked as kids were much, much creepier than we thought.
< You know that feeling.
A toy that looks wrong now. A cartoon you can't watch as an adult.
Candle Cove takes that feeling and gives it a villain.

> Theory three. The gaps.
A researcher who studies creepypasta had a view of this.
She pointed to the story's open-endedness.
The show is barely explained.
That leaves room for every reader to build their own version.
Their own Skin-Taker. Their own Laughingstock.
And their own answer to what the static means.
She also said this kind of horror is common in creepypasta.
It claims to give you a glimpse of a terrible truth.
< One that cannot be unseen.
That may infect you, contaminate you, and haunt you forever.
She tied it to a current revival of Lovecraft-style fear.
Horror that comes from a vast, indifferent universe, rather than any single monster.

> Notice what all three theories share.
The story works because it isn't finished.
Straub left the door open.
And a million other people walked through it.

> Now, back to the static.
What was the boy watching?
The story won't say.
Maybe the show was real to him, and invisible to his mother.
Maybe he invented every pirate, one by one, from noise.
< Or maybe a whole group of adults has built one imaginary show from a single child's fantasy.
Which, if you think about it, is exactly what happened to the story itself.
A bit of static. And a lot of people filling it in.

> And the legend kept going.
In 2015, Straub self-published a collection of short stories.
It was called Candle Cove and Other Stories.
The same year, the Syfy channel announced it would adapt the story.
As the first season of a new series called Channel Zero.
The season took the name Candle Cove.
It expanded on the story.
It centers on a child psychologist who returns home to investigate a mystery from the 1980s.
Paul Schneider and Fiona Shaw starred.
It premiered on October 11, 2016.
One report noted that the show appeared to credit Straub for inspiring it.
And it included a kid made of teeth.
[pause 0.7]
> And there's another spinoff.
An analog horror series called Local 58.
It's built on the same idea: a broadcast that shouldn't exist.

> So let's sum up.
A man reads a joke about a nightmare.
He writes a fake forum thread about a fake show.
Strangers copy it, repost it, and act it out.
Fans invent seasons, a book from 1767, and fake footage.
A television network turns it into a real series.
< And through all of it, there never was a Channel 58 pirate.
Only the memory.
[pause 0.7]
> Maybe that's the real horror of Candle Cove.
Not a skeleton in a top hat.
< But how easily a crowd can agree on something that was never there.
And how good it feels to say, "Yes. I remember that too."

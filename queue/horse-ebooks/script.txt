> Let's go back to how it all started.
> A Twitter account named Horse_ebooks.
> Apparently intended to promote e-books.
> But what it actually posted were amusing non sequiturs.
> Cryptic missives.
> Fragments of modified text copied from other sources.
> Mixed with occasional promotional links to websites selling e-books.
> Associated with the affiliate marketing company ClickBank.

> Look at some of the lines it spat out:
> "I will make certain you never buy knives again,"
> "We all agree, no one looks cool,"
> "Is the dance floor calling? No,"
> "Unfortunately, as you probably already know, people"
> And of course:
> "Everything happens so much"

> How did it generate this strange poetry?
> Investigators at Splitsider and Gawker pieced together the puzzle.
> The creator was believed to be a web developer and spammer based in Russia.
> Named Alexei Kouznetsov, also romanized as Alexey Kuznetsov.
> He owned as many as 170 domains associated with similar efforts.
> Other accounts in the network included companyebooks, action_ebooks, and mystery_ebooks.
> The bot pulled snippets of text from books and other sources.
> Scrambling them together.
> Apparently in an effort to evade spam detection.

> But here is what made Horse_ebooks different from the rest.
> Unlike many other Twitter spam accounts, it did not employ typical spammer techniques.
> No mass-following.
> No unsolicited replies to Twitter users.
> Because it avoided those classic moves, the account was never closed down the way Twitter spam accounts frequently are.
> Instead, it sat there.
> Spitting out surreal fragments about horses and other things.
> Becoming a never-ending fountain of inspiration and amusement for a certain sliver of the internet.

> Artists took notice.
> Burton Durand started a Tumblr blog called Horse E-Comics, featuring comic strips inspired by the account.
> Other artists posted drawings based on messages like "leg butt" or "Too many horses. Throw a horse away."
> Fans created fan art, fan fiction, and unofficial merchandise like T-shirts.
> UGO Networks named it one of the best Twitter feeds in 2011.
> Time.com followed suit in 2012.
> John Herrman wrote at Splitsider that Horse_ebooks "might be the best Twitter account that has ever existed."
> The Atlantic eventually called it "the most successful piece of cyber fiction."

> But that was not the strangest part.
> Because behind the curtain, a quiet takeover had already happened.
> On September 14, 2011, followers noticed something odd.
> The account began tweeting "via web" instead of "via Horse ebooks".
> The frequency of tweets promoting ClickBank significantly dropped.
> Meanwhile, the number of funny tweets increased.
> Many followers speculated wildly.
> Had the spam algorithm been changed?
> Had a hacker acquired the account's password?
> Was a different person running it now?

> The answers stayed hidden for two years.
> Until September 24, 2013.
> That was the day everything clicked into place.
> It was revealed that the @Horse_ebooks account had actually been sold back in 2011.
> The buyer?
> An employee at BuzzFeed named Jacob Bakkila.
> Bakkila had approached Kuznetsov in 2011 with the intent of buying the account.
> Kuznetsov agreed.
> And since 2011, Horse_ebooks had been operated by Bakkila.

> It was all part of a multi-year performance art piece.
> Staged as an alternate reality game developed for viral marketing.
> Toward a larger art project by the art collective Synydyne.
> Synydyne was formed in 2006, led by internet artists Thomas Bender and Jacob Bakkila.
> Their projects included Horse_ebooks, Bear Stearns Bravo, Pronunciation Book, and This is My Milwaukee.
> Bear Stearns Bravo was a series of interactive videos about the 2007 subprime mortgage financial crisis.
> In a 2015 interview, Bakkila explained the collective's approach:
> "Most of what Synydyne has created thus far is either designed to spread as quickly as possible—@Horse_ebooks and Pronunciation Book being the obvious examples—or to be as difficult as possible to access."

> On the exact day of the big reveal in September 2013, the Twitter account stopped updating entirely.
> Bakkila, along with others who contributed to the project, performed at an art installation.
> Fans could call in and have various horse_ebooks tweets read to them.
> The Independent reported that Twitter was 'devastated' by the revelation that the beloved bot was actually human-run.
> What felt like a glitching machine, a mechanical accident of the internet age, turned out to be a carefully crafted work of cyber fiction.
